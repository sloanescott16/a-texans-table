Grillades and grits

Sunday, Louisiana week, tonight's dinner only. Active time: about 2 hours, from 4:26 for 6:30, with no real hands-off stretch. The roux is the long pole: a fat-heavy roux takes about 70 minutes to reach copper at 325 to 345°F and wants a stir every minute or two, and the medallions, the sear, the Instant Pot and the grits fit around it. With a jar of copper roux already in the fridge it drops to about 1 hour. A beef chuck tender roast sliced across the grain into medallions and pounded thin, seared hard in the cast iron, pressure cooked with its juices, beef stock and tomatoes, then married into a copper roux gravy with the trinity, over cheddar grits. Half the roux goes into a pint jar for Monday's gumbo at no extra work.

Notes

Equipment: 12 inch cast iron skillet for the sear · 6 quart Instant Pot · Dutch oven for the roux and the gravy · big pot, 5 to 6 quart, for the grits · heatproof steel or glass bowl for the hot roux, and a pint jar · ladle and whisk · meat mallet or rolling pin · infrared thermometer · probe thermometer.

Instant Pot: the medallions and their plate juices go on the bottom, the stock jug is poured over and the tomatoes go on top. Do not stir: tomatoes or anything thick on the bottom trips the burn warning. About 10 minutes to come to pressure, 20 minutes at High, 10 minutes natural release. Never pressure cook a roux-thickened liquid: it scorches. The roux base waits in the Dutch oven and the hot liquid is whisked into it after.

Long route, Dutch oven braise: with the afternoon free, skip the Instant Pot. After the spices, whisk the stock jug into the roux base a cup at a time, tomatoes and bay leaves in, medallions and juices back, the Dutch oven's own lid on, and braise 2 hours at 180 to 190°F on a probe, stirring and turning at the hour and the hour and a half, until a fork slides out with no grip. Start about 1 hour 45 minutes earlier; the 2 hours are hands off.

Monday's roux: the roux is made double. At copper, half goes into the heatproof bowl to cool, then into a pint jar in the fridge for Monday's gumbo, about 205 g. That is the only thing tonight does for another night. The chicken broth is its own page.

Roux safety: a roux near 350°F sticks to skin. Long sleeves, no water near the pan, and stir with a flat wooden spatula. Never pour it hot into a cold glass jar: it cools in the steel or glass bowl first. Black specks or a sharp, acrid burnt smell mean start again; a scorched roux cannot be saved.

Roux heat: working band 325 to 350°F on the infrared, read on the roux itself. 375°F is the red line; with olive oil in the fat, 360°F is the warning. Beef tallow is the default fat, with canola as the alternative; Sunday's was 105 g tallow, 110 g canola and 55 g olive oil to 155 g flour. A fat-heavy roux, equal cups of fat and flour rather than equal grams, reads lighter than it is: Sunday's took about 70 minutes to reach copper at 325 to 345°F. Copper is right for grillades; there is no need to push to dark chocolate. If it darkens faster than the cues, lower the burner: colour is the cue, the clock is the guide. Chilled, a tallow roux sets hard: that is normal, and it melts straight back in when warmed. The roux is always made in the Dutch oven, never the cast iron skillet.

Food safety: pressure cooked chuck tender is far past the 145°F safe point; the fork test is the cue for tender. Leftovers into the fridge within 2 hours, moved out of the Dutch oven into a container first.

Grits: about 35 minutes all in: about 10 minutes for 6 cups of water to come to the boil, then 25 to cook. His batch is 240 g grits to 6 cups water at 3 servings. Regular or quick grits, not instant.

Green beans: left off. Sunday's cook had no room for them. Another time: frozen green beans, steamed from frozen in a small saucepan for 7 minutes, no trimming.

Size: set for 3 servings, one beef chuck tender roast of about 2.75 lb in three sear batches. At 5 or 6, sear in four batches and set Eating at 6 minutes later; a 6 quart Instant Pot holds it.

Mild for the boys: the Cajun seasoning amount keeps it gentle and nothing else hot goes in the pot. Hot sauce stays on the table for the Captain.

Pepper: never on raw meat before a sear. Pepper goes on each seared face after it turns up, and on the last side as it comes off.

No Cajun seasoning? For each 1 tsp: 1/2 tsp smoked paprika, 1/4 tsp chili powder, 1/8 tsp dried oregano, 1/8 tsp ground cumin and a pinch of kosher salt.

No thyme? Use the same amount of dried oregano.

No hot sauce? A pinch of chili powder over his bowl, or a pinch of chili powder stirred into a spoon of cider vinegar.

Salt: the kosher salt amounts suit a light, flaky kosher salt. A dense, fine kosher salt is about twice as salty by volume: use half.

Infrared readings: infrared reads the surface only. Cast iron reads true; a shiny saucepan reads low, so aim at the oil or the food. Aim close, straight down, and read two or three spots, as pans vary from edge to centre. Liquids (stock, gravy, sauce, grits) go by a probe.
